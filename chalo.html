Plan smarter. Travel easier.

A guided journey planner for Pune public transport

8:45 am.

Person A needs to get from Savannah to Tea Villa Café. Google Maps says 43 minutes, three modes, and one interchange she has never used.

She has the metro app on her phone. She doesn’t open it.

02The Problem

Person A isn’t unusual.

74.4%

of Pune commuters plan their journeys in Google Maps instead of the official metro app.

The transport authority’s own product has already lost the planning moment. Whatever gets built has to win it back at the point where the trip is decided, not after the rider is on a platform.

51.2%

avoid the metro entirely because stations are too far from where they are actually going.

This is not a metro problem. It is a first-and-last-mile problem that the metro app never had a way to answer, so half the potential riders never got on.

03Research

Where the city is losing riders

I started with desk research, expecting a usability problem. What I found was a geography and cost problem that no interface had been asked to solve.

DISTANCE 30–45 min

Some destinations sit that far from the nearest metro station, requiring an additional rickshaw or bus ride.

So the metro is never the whole trip.

COST 10–15%

of monthly budget is what transport costs reach, pushing lower-income commuters away from metro-plus-auto combinations entirely.

So the extra leg has to be priced, not hidden.

RESULT 1.55–2.5 L
06 lakh target

daily riders, against a projected target of 6 lakh.

A system built for three times the ridership it has.

So I asked riders what stops them

A survey of Pune commuters. I expected distance to dominate. It came second.

62.8% use the metro only sometimes. It exists in their mental map, but it has not become the default way to move. 51.2% avoid it because stations are too far from their final destination — the desk research, confirmed by the people living it.

Riders described unclear interchanges, unfamiliar routes and last-mile connectivity as the points where they stop trusting the plan — and start asking other passengers for help.

Distance is the barrier people can name. Not knowing how the system works is the barrier that actually keeps them off it.

05Core Problems

Three edges to fix

Affinity mapping the research produced eleven overlapping themes. Stripped of synonyms, three remained.

01

Not knowing the route

44% named new-city confusion as their single biggest barrier, and 62.8% ride only sometimes.

Cost to the rider: the trip gets planned in another app, or not by public transport at all.

02

Last-mile blindness

51.2% avoid the metro over station distance; some destinations are 30–45 minutes past the last stop.

Cost to the rider: an unplanned, unpriced auto at the end of a journey that was supposed to be cheap.

03

No live feedback

14% cited having no awareness of timings or schedules once they were in the system.

Cost to the rider: the journey becomes dependent on asking other passengers for help.

06Ideation

Three concepts, one right unit

CONCEPT 01REJECTED

Step-by-step guide

Plan metro and bus journeys step by step: best route, direction, platform, stops and total fare, with QR ticketing and auto or cab booking bolted on for the first and last mile.

Note

Why not: strong once you have chosen a route, silent about whether public transport was the right call at all. It could not answer the 51.2% who had already written the metro off.

CONCEPT 02SELECTED

Door-to-door planner

A first-time-user-friendly planner covering metro, bus, auto and cab, ranking every available route by time, cost and savings against a direct cab, with step-by-step guidance, live tracking, ticketing, stop alerts and exit directions.

Why I chose this

Why this one: it treats the door-to-door trip as the unit, which is the only shape that answers last-mile blindness. Ranking by cost gives the 44% who are new to the city a basis for judging options they have never used.

CONCEPT 03REJECTED

Minimal quick-search

Search stations or areas fast, compare fares, book in a tap, with nearby landmarks, recent searches and saved trips making repeat travel quicker.

Note

Why not: recents and saved trips are built for someone who already knows the network. That is the confident commuter, not the rider the research kept describing.

EDGE 01 — NOT KNOWING THE ROUTE

Route Planning

Person A types two places. They gets three whole journeys, not three metro lines, each priced end to end and ranked against the cab she would otherwise take.

Why I chose thisThe decision: I put the savings chip on the card, not in the detail view. Transport reaching 10–15% of monthly budget is why people stop considering multi-modal at all, so the number that answers that objection has to be visible before anyone taps.

The top card is labelled Best Pick rather than just placed first. Someone new to the city has no basis to read an ordered list.

Route planning — three whole journeys, each priced end to end and ranked against the cab.

Live navigation — the ticket prompt fires three minutes before arrival.

EDGE 02 — THE WALK TO THE STATION

Live Navigation

Turn-by-turn to the station entrance, with the incoming metro named on the same card as the distance left to walk.

Why I chose thisThe decision: The ticket prompt fires three minutes before arrival. Earlier felt like nagging; later felt rushed. Three minutes is long enough to pay while walking and short enough that the train is real.

The confirmation lands as the walk ends, so Jia reaches the gate already holding a QR code.

EDGE 03 — THE QUEUE AT THE GATE

Ticket Booking

Stations, date, departure and platform arrive pre-filled from the plan. Person A adds a name and a number, and sees the total before she pays.

Why I chose thisThe decision: One screen, not a wizard. Splitting passenger details, OTP and payment into steps reads well at a desk, but this form is filled during a five-minute walk, every extra screen is a chance to lose the train.

The e-ticket keeps platform, departure and status beside the QR, so nothing has to be remembered at the gate.

Ticket booking — one screen, not a wizard.

Live tracking — stops remaining is the headline number, not ETA.

EDGE 04 — THE LAST MILE

Live Tracking

On the Aqua Line, Person A sees where she is in the sequence and can book the auto that will be waiting at Wakad before they stands up.

Why I chose thisThe decision: Stops remaining is the headline number, not ETA. On a line you have never ridden the question is not when, it is which stop is mine and a count of two out of eight answers it without reading the list.

The journey closes on a summary that leads with the exit-scan reminder, because the QR is needed at both gates.

10Reflection

What this project changed for me.

What surprised me was how little of the problem sat inside the metro. Almost every frustration people described happened before they reached a platform or after they left one and none of it was visible in the app that was supposed to help.
